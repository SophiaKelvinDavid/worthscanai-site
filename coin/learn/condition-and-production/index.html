Guide 02 · Examine the evidence

Condition & production

Once the issue is identified, collectors examine what happened when the coin was made and what happened afterward. Wear, strike, surfaces, and damage describe different evidence.

WorthScan AI can offer preliminary visual observations; it does not provide official coin grading.

Circulation wear and preliminary condition

Circulation gradually removes metal from the highest points of a design. Look for flattened hair, lettering, feathers, leaves, rims, or other raised details known for that coin type. Even wear across expected high points differs from one isolated scrape.

A photo-based review can describe visible preservation, but professional grading also considers factors that images may conceal. Lighting, focus, magnification, cleaning, and surface alteration can all change how a coin appears.

Luster, marks, and damage

LusterLight reflected by microscopic flow lines created during striking; it can diminish through wear or cleaning.
Contact marksNicks or abrasions from coins touching during production, storage, or circulation.
Normal wearGradual loss of high-point detail consistent with handling and use.
DamageBends, holes, deep scratches, corrosion, harsh cleaning, or other changes beyond ordinary wear.

Do not clean a coin to make it look brighter. Abrasive polishing and chemical dipping can permanently alter its surface and collector appeal.

Composition changes the identification

Similar designs may exist in copper, nickel, silver, gold, clad layers, plated metals, or other alloys. Weight, diameter, edge, color, and a documented specification can help distinguish formats. For example, some 1976 U.S. commemorative-design coins were made in both copper-nickel clad and special silver-clad versions.

Use safe measurements

A non-destructive weight and diameter check can support an attribution. Avoid acid tests, scratches, cutting, or other tests that damage the coin.

Strike quality is not the same as wear

A coin can leave the press with weakly formed details because of die state, pressure, centering, planchet preparation, or the relief of the design. That weakness may resemble circulation wear. Compare the entire coin: original luster around a weak detail can indicate a soft strike, while friction on exposed high points can indicate wear.

Proof versus circulation strike

“Proof” describes a special method of manufacture, not a condition grade. Proof coins are generally made for collectors with specially prepared dies and planchets. Circulation strikes are made for ordinary monetary use. A proof can be impaired, spotted, or worn, and a circulation strike can be exceptionally well preserved.

Why small condition differences matter

Collector demand often concentrates on scarce coins at particular preservation levels. Two authentic examples of the exact same issue can therefore sell for radically different amounts. Certification, surface originality, eye appeal, strike, and market timing may all contribute.

Important: A preliminary visual condition assessment is not a substitute for authentication or grading by a recognized professional service when a coin may be rare, altered, counterfeit, or high value.
